# Fasting plasma glucose: general interpretation

<!-- vitamap:block summary -->
## In one sentence

Fasting glucose measures the amount of glucose in the blood at one moment
after at least eight hours without food or drinks other than water.

<!-- vitamap:block analogy -->
## A picture to begin with

Imagine the body as a city and glucose as one of the fuels moving through it.
A fasting measurement is like observing traffic before breakfast: no energy
from a recent meal is arriving, but the city has not shut down and continues
to regulate its supplies.

Literally, blood carries glucose, the liver can release it, and several
hormones help regulate it. The picture explains the timing of the measurement;
it does not represent the whole of metabolism.

<!-- vitamap:block literal -->
## What it actually means

Fasting plasma glucose is a snapshot. NIDDK explains that it is usually tested
in the morning after a fast of at least eight hours and is used to detect and
help diagnose disorders of glucose regulation.

For nonpregnant adults, NIDDK lists these general references, attributed to
American Diabetes Association criteria:

| Fasting plasma glucose | General classification |
|---|---|
| 99 mg/dL or below | normal range |
| 100–125 mg/dL | prediabetes range |
| 126 mg/dL or above | diabetes range; normally requires confirmation |

These thresholds are general screening and diagnostic criteria, not an
automatic individual interpretation or a substitute for the range shown in a
specific laboratory report.

<!-- vitamap:block relations -->
## How it relates to other results

Fasting glucose and HbA1c look at different time scales. The first measures one
moment; HbA1c approximates average glucose exposure over the preceding months.
They can provide complementary information and do not always classify the same
person in the same way.

An incomplete fast, acute illness, stress, some medicines, pregnancy,
biological variation, and sample processing can affect the result. Date,
conditions, unit, method, and source report should therefore be preserved.

<!-- vitamap:block limitations -->
## Limits of this explanation

The city is never truly “without traffic”: body activity, hormonal regulation,
and glucose release continue during fasting. The metaphor does not represent
differences between tissues or identify why a particular result is higher or
lower.

One result does not establish a diagnosis, determine a diabetes type, or
describe glucose throughout the day. This card neither interprets a personal
result nor identifies a cause. The test also does not directly measure how
well the pancreas or liver works; those organs participate in regulation, but
the reported measurement is plasma glucose.

<!-- vitamap:block deep_dive -->
## If you want to go deeper

During fasting, regulated processes involving the liver, pancreas, and several
hormones keep glucose available. Insulin is important, but regulation cannot
be reduced to a single key that opens every cell.

NIDDK also notes biological and methodological variation in fasting glucose
and the importance of correct sample processing. Two nearby results should not
be presented as a definite trend without checking comparability, units, and
measurement conditions.
