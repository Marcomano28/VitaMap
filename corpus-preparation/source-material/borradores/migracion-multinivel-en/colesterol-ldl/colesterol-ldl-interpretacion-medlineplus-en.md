# LDL cholesterol: general interpretation

<!-- vitamap:block summary -->
## In one sentence

LDL names a class of lipoproteins: particles that carry cholesterol and other
lipids through the blood. The laboratory result usually reports how much
cholesterol is being carried in those particles, expressed as LDL-C.

<!-- vitamap:block analogy -->
## A picture to begin with

Imagine the circulation as a road network and lipoproteins as vehicles able to
carry a load that cannot travel freely through a watery environment. LDL
describes one class of those vehicles; the number in the report refers to
their cholesterol cargo, not to a separate substance called “bad cholesterol.”

The picture helps explain transport, but it does not show the particles’ real
size or number and cannot reveal what is happening in a particular person’s
arteries.

<!-- vitamap:block literal -->
## What it actually means

The body needs cholesterol for functions that include building cell membranes
and hormones. Because lipids do not dissolve well in blood, they circulate in
lipoproteins. LDL means *low-density lipoprotein*.

MedlinePlus gives a general healthy LDL reference of under 100 mg/dL for
adults. It also explains that the appropriate level for an individual may
depend on age, family history, lifestyle, and other cardiovascular risk
factors. The range printed by a laboratory and goals agreed in clinical care
may differ from this general educational reference.

<!-- vitamap:block relations -->
## How it relates to other results

LDL is part of a lipid profile and is read alongside total cholesterol, HDL,
non-HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides. Sharing a panel does not mean that one
marker automatically causes another; each value describes a different aspect
of lipid transport.

The LDL result may be measured directly or calculated from other parts of the
lipid profile. Method, unit, and report context therefore matter when results
are compared.

<!-- vitamap:block limitations -->
## Limits of this explanation

“Good” and “bad” are educational shortcuts. They do not mean that HDL is
always protective or that an LDL particle is harmful by definition. The
vehicle metaphor also leaves out much of lipoprotein metabolism.

A single LDL result does not diagnose atherosclerosis, calculate overall
cardiovascular risk on its own, or determine whether treatment is needed. This
card explains the marker in general; individual interpretation requires the
full report and clinical context.

<!-- vitamap:block deep_dive -->
## If you want to go deeper

LDL-C describes the cholesterol contained in LDL particles; it does not count
the particles directly. Other markers, including non-HDL cholesterol and
apolipoprotein B, answer related but non-interchangeable questions. Measures
from the same biological system should not be treated as equivalent.

Concentrations may be reported in mg/dL or mmol/L. A safe comparison over time
must preserve method, unit, and provenance; VitaMap must not merge values in
different units without an explicit, validated conversion.
